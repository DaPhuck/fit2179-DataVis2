Prologue · The Dark Sky Advantage

Why Australia?

Australia sits almost entirely in the southern hemisphere, granting exclusive views of the Milky Way's galactic core, the Magellanic Clouds, and celestial objects invisible from most of the world. Its vast, arid interior places millions of square kilometres far from city lights — among the darkest skies on the planet. This visualisation takes you through five lenses: the solar engine, geomagnetic storms and aurora, a once-in-twenty-years superstorm, rocks from space, and eclipses. Everything is anchored to the Australian landscape and sky.
Source Globe at Night (n.d.); Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS] (2021)

In the city you might count a few dozen stars; in the outback, thousands. This map shades every state by how dark its skies really are, drawn from citizen-science readings. The pattern is instant: a bright, populated coastal fringe — and a vast, pristine interior. State averages come from citizen observations, biased toward populated areas, so they understate the true darkness of remote interiors.

Section 1 — Solar Engine

The solar engine — what drives it all

Every phenomenon in this story — aurora, geomagnetic storms, even enhanced meteor activity — traces back to one source: the sun. Our star breathes on an 11-year cycle of rising and falling magnetic chaos, measured by the count of dark sunspots on its surface. We are currently in Solar Cycle 25, approaching its predicted peak in 2025–2026. Right now, the sun is as active as it has been in two decades — and that energy is flowing toward Earth.
Source World Data Center SILSO, Royal Observatory of Belgium (2015)
Source Matzka et al. (2022)

Every story here starts with the sun. Its magnetic activity rises and falls on an ~11-year heartbeat, counted in dark sunspots — quiet years, then a roaring peak, over and over. We're now near the crest of Solar Cycle 25, the busiest sun in two decades. That energy is what flows toward Earth in everything that follows.

When the sun is loud, Earth feels it. This chart counts the days each year that geomagnetic storms rattle our magnetic field, measured by the Kp index — and the rhythm echoes the sunspot cycle almost beat for beat. The rare severe days (Kp ≥ 7) are when the aurora pushes far enough north to light up Australian skies.

Cycles aren't equal SC24 was notably weaker than SC22 or SC23; not every peak hits the same height.
We're at the crest SC25 (amber band) is peaking around 2024–25, the strongest activity since the 1990s.
Storms follow the sun Storm-day counts peak near each solar maximum and collapse in the minima — the same pattern as the sun's activity.
2024 stands out The recent SC25 rise delivered the May superstorm, the strongest in 20 years.

Section 2 — Storm Seasons

Storm seasons — when to look up

Solar activity sets the long-run average — but within any given year, geomagnetic storms are not distributed evenly across months. The "equinox effect" means that March and September consistently produce more severe storms than midsummer or midwinter. This counterintuitive fact — rooted in the geometry of Earth's magnetic field orientation relative to the sun — has enormous practical value for aurora chasers planning a trip south. The following charts decode the seasonality hidden inside nine decades of geomagnetic records.

If you're planning just one trip south to chase the aurora, timing matters as much as luck. Severe storm days aren't spread evenly across the year — they cluster around the equinoxes.

Section 3 — May 2024 Superstorm

Case study — the May 2024 superstorm

On the night of 10 May 2024, the strongest geomagnetic storm in 20 years struck Earth. With a peak Kp of 9, the aurora australis blazed across the entire southern sky — visible from every Australian state, reaching as far north as Queensland. The ARCTICS survey captured 696 observers across 30+ countries. This section zooms into the Australian respondents, turning a historic storm into a human story: who saw it, from where, what they described, and what the data reveals about aurora visibility thresholds across latitudes.

On 10 May 2024, the strongest geomagnetic storm in two decades lit the southern sky. Mapping where ARCTICS respondents stood when they looked up reveals a clear geography to the night — and shows just how far north the aurora pushed.

This storm broke the rule At Kp ≈ 9, the aurora reached far enough north to be seen toward Queensland — a reach that normally takes a once-in-decades storm.

Section 4 — Rocks From Space

Rocks from space — fireballs & meteorites

Not everything falling from the sky is light. Every day, tonnes of rocky debris left over from the solar system's formation rain down on Earth. Most vaporise in a streak of light — fireballs bright enough to cast shadows. A precious few survive the fiery descent and land as meteorites. Australia's vast, dry, geologically stable interior is one of the planet's finest meteorite-preserving environments — the continent is, in a very real sense, a natural meteorite museum.
Sources Center for Near Earth Object Studies [CNEOS] (n.d.); Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS] (2021)
Sources The Meteoritical Society (2024); Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS] (2021)

Most space rock arrives as light, not stone. This map plots two decades of NASA CNEOS bolide detections over Australia — each circle sized by the energy it released, coloured by how high it burned. Bright entries scatter right across the continent, from desert to coast.

A precious few survive the fall. These recoveries cluster tightly in the arid south and interior — not where more rock lands, but where the dry, bare ground preserves stones and makes them findable. Teal stony finds dominate; the lone amber giant is the Windimurra iron.

Light scatters, stone clusters Fireballs are detected continent-wide; recovered meteorites bunch in the arid south — the core contrast of the section.
The outback preserves Dry, stable, sparsely vegetated ground is why finds concentrate inland, not because more falls there.
Stony dominates the ground Recovered finds are overwhelmingly stony (teal), with iron (e.g. Windimurra, 30,000 g) the rare exception.
Energy spans orders of magnitude Fireball circle sizes range hugely; a few high-energy entries dwarf the rest.

Section 5 — Eclipses

Eclipses — Australia's appointments with shadow

Total solar eclipses rank among the rarest and most awe-inspiring events a human being can witness — a moment when day turns to twilight, stars appear at noon, and the solar corona shimmers around a perfect black disc. Australia is fortunate: several total solar eclipses will cross the continent in the coming decades, including Sydney in 2028 and Queensland in 2038. This section maps every eclipse visible from Australia across 160 years — past and future.

Section 6 — Dark Sky Finder

Finding your dark sky — where to go tonight

Armed with an understanding of what to look for and when, the final question is: where?
